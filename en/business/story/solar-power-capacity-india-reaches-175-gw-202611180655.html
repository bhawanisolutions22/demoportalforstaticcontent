India’s Solar Power Capacity Reaches 175 GW in a Decade

India’s solar energy sector has recorded remarkable growth over the past decade, transforming from a relatively small contributor to one of the country's most important sources of renewable electricity. Installed solar capacity has increased from just 2.82 GW in 2014 to approximately 175 GW as of October 31, 2026, representing nearly 62 times growth.

This expansion has been driven by government initiatives, investments in large-scale solar projects, improvements in domestic manufacturing, competitive electricity tariffs and increasing participation from households and farmers.

Solar energy is also becoming more accessible beyond large power plants. Rooftop installations, solar-powered irrigation pumps, floating solar projects and off-grid systems are extending clean electricity solutions to different parts of the country.

India's growing renewable energy capabilities and investment opportunities were among the major highlights of the Bharat Renewable Energy Summit & Expo (BRESE) 2026, organised at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi from November 2 to 5.

India's Solar Journey: From 2.82 GW to 175 GW

In 2014, India's installed solar capacity stood at only 2.82 GW. Since then, sustained policy support and private investment have helped the country build a large solar energy ecosystem.

India added approximately 37 GW of solar capacity during 2025, surpassing the United States in annual additions and becoming the world's second-largest market for solar capacity growth.

The pace of expansion accelerated further during FY 2025–26, when the country installed a record 44.61 GW of solar capacity, compared with 23.83 GW in FY 2024–25.

During the ongoing FY 2026–27, another 24.29 GW had already been added at the time of the reported figures.

Solar energy's growth is part of India's wider transition towards non-fossil electricity generation. As of September 30, 2026, the country's total installed non-fossil fuel capacity reached 308.03 GW. This included 299.25 GW of renewable energy and 8.78 GW of nuclear power.

India also recorded its highest annual addition of non-fossil fuel capacity in FY 2025–26, adding 55.29 GW.

An important milestone was achieved on July 29, 2025, when renewable energy sources met 51.5% of India's electricity demand, marking the highest monthly contribution recorded up to that time.

Where India's Solar Capacity Is Installed

Solar installations have expanded across several Indian states, with Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra emerging as major contributors.

Large utility-scale solar projects account for approximately 133 GW of installed capacity, including the solar component of hybrid projects. Grid-connected rooftop systems contribute another 34.97 GW, while the remaining capacity comes from off-grid installations.

Some of India's prominent solar developments include:

  • Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan
  • Pavagada Solar Park in Karnataka
  • Ananthapuramu Solar Park in Andhra Pradesh
  • Kurnool Solar Park in Andhra Pradesh
  • Rewa Ultra Mega Solar Park in Madhya Pradesh

Hybrid renewable energy projects combine solar power with battery storage or other electricity sources to improve power availability. Off-grid installations provide electricity in locations where conventional grid connectivity is limited or unavailable.

Government Policies Driving Solar Energy Expansion

India's solar development has been supported by a combination of national programmes, climate commitments, investment incentives and infrastructure initiatives.

The National Solar Mission, launched in 2010, established an early foundation for large-scale solar deployment. Subsequent programmes, including the Solar Parks Scheme and Green Energy Corridor Programme, helped accelerate project development and improve electricity transmission.

At the COP26 climate conference in Glasgow, India announced its Panchamrit commitments. These included achieving 500 GW of non-fossil fuel energy capacity by 2030 and reaching net-zero emissions by 2070.

These commitments were subsequently reflected in India's updated Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement.

Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment) complements these initiatives by encouraging citizens to adopt sustainable lifestyles and environmentally responsible consumption practices.

Renewable Purchase Obligations Support Long-Term Demand

Renewable Purchase Obligations (RPOs) are another important part of India's renewable energy framework.

Under these obligations, electricity distribution companies and other designated entities must purchase a specified percentage of their electricity from renewable sources.

This requirement creates predictable demand for renewable electricity, providing greater certainty for developers and investors.

The notified RPO trajectory extends through 2029–30, with the target for FY 2026–27 set at 35.95%.

Government programmes are also encouraging solar adoption among individual consumers and farmers. PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana supports residential rooftop installations, while PM-KUSUM promotes solar-powered irrigation and agricultural feeder solarisation.

Competitive Bidding Makes Solar Electricity More Affordable

The declining cost of solar electricity has played a major role in India's renewable energy expansion.

Competitive bidding and e-Reverse Auction mechanisms allow project developers to offer electricity at increasingly competitive tariffs. Procurement agencies such as the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI) have helped expand this transparent procurement process.

Solar electricity tariffs have fallen from approximately ₹18 per unit in 2010 to a record-low ₹1.99 per unit, achieved by Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Limited in 2020.

This reduction has strengthened the commercial viability of solar projects and helped India become one of the world's lowest-cost markets for solar electricity.

According to the reported figures, the Levelised Cost of Electricity (LCOE) for utility-scale solar photovoltaic projects in India stood at USD 35 per MWh in 2025, compared with the global average of USD 44 per MWh.

Solar Parks Provide Land and Infrastructure for Large Projects

Large solar parks have helped developers overcome challenges related to land availability, electricity transmission and common infrastructure.

The Solar Parks Scheme was initially introduced with a target of 20 GW, which was subsequently increased to 40 GW in 2017.

As of October 2026, a total of 56 solar parks with a combined sanctioned capacity of 39,461 MW had been approved across India.

Gujarat had seven approved parks with a sanctioned capacity of 12,150 MW, while Rajasthan had 11 parks with 10,726 MW.

Other major contributors included:

  • Madhya Pradesh: Nine parks with 4,208 MW
  • Andhra Pradesh: Five parks with 4,200 MW
  • Uttar Pradesh: Eight parks with 3,740 MW

Solar park development has also been approved in Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Karnataka, Kerala, Maharashtra, Mizoram and Odisha. An inter-state floating solar park spans Jharkhand and West Bengal.

Bhadla Solar Park: A Major Solar Energy Hub

Located in Rajasthan's arid Bhadla region, Bhadla Solar Park is among the world's largest solar power complexes.

Its development began in 2015 under the Solar Parks Scheme and was completed in phases by 2020.

Spread across approximately 5,700 hectares, equivalent to 56 square kilometres, the park has an installed capacity of 2,245 MW.

The region's high solar irradiation, low rainfall and sparse vegetation make it suitable for large-scale solar electricity generation.

The availability of common infrastructure, including prepared land and transmission facilities, helped accelerate construction and attract private investment.

Competitive bidding at the park also contributed to some of India's lowest solar electricity tariffs, demonstrating how large solar parks can reduce project costs and support rapid capacity expansion.

Green Energy Corridors Improve Electricity Transmission

Increasing renewable energy generation requires an equally strong electricity transmission network.

Solar-rich regions are often located far from major electricity consumption centres. The Green Energy Corridor (GEC) Programme addresses this challenge by strengthening transmission infrastructure and enabling renewable electricity to reach the national grid.

As of February 2026, the programme had facilitated the evacuation of 24,567.8 MW of renewable energy through improved transmission networks.

Projects across ten states are planned to support the evacuation of approximately 44 GW of renewable electricity.

These transmission facilities will become increasingly important as India adds more solar, wind and other renewable energy capacity.

Floating Solar and Battery Storage Create New Opportunities

India is also exploring floating solar photovoltaic projects to make better use of reservoirs and other suitable water bodies.

On July 31, 2026, the Government approved the Pradhan Mantri Surya Sarovar Yojana (PM-SSY), with a financial outlay of ₹5,070 crore.

The programme aims to support 5,000 MW of floating solar photovoltaic capacity with co-located Energy Storage Systems.

The storage component will have a minimum capacity of two hours, equivalent to 10,000 MWh. This is expected to help states manage peak electricity demand and improve the reliability of renewable power.

The scheme will be implemented from FY 2026–27 to FY 2030–31 and will be available to all states and Union Territories.

By using existing reservoirs and industrial ponds, floating solar projects can reduce pressure on land resources while expanding renewable electricity generation.

The National Institute of Solar Energy estimates India's floating solar potential at approximately 102.18 GWp across reservoirs and suitable inland water bodies.

The initiative is expected to:

  • Reduce approximately 10 million tonnes of carbon dioxide emissions annually.
  • Generate around 16,000–17,000 full-time equivalent jobs.
  • Encourage domestic manufacturing of floating solar systems, photovoltaic cells and modules.
  • Support the development of energy storage equipment.
  • Increase India's floating solar capacity by 5,000 MW from its existing level of approximately 800 MW.

Domestic Solar Manufacturing Capacity Expands

Alongside increasing solar installations, India has focused on developing domestic manufacturing capabilities to reduce dependence on imported equipment.

Solar module manufacturing capacity has grown substantially, increasing from 2.3 GW in 2014 to approximately 172 GW as of March 31, 2026, and further to 192 GW by June 2026.

Two major government interventions have supported this expansion: the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme for High-Efficiency Solar PV Modules and the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM).

Production Linked Incentive Scheme

Launched in 2021 with an initial allocation of ₹4,500 crore, the PLI Scheme for High-Efficiency Solar PV Modules was expanded in 2022 with an additional ₹19,500 crore.

The programme aims to encourage domestic solar manufacturing, strengthen the supply chain and reduce import dependence.

By October 2024, the scheme had attracted investments of approximately ₹35,000 crore and generated direct employment for around 10,000 people.

Approved List of Models and Manufacturers

Introduced in 2019, the ALMM framework establishes quality and eligibility requirements for solar photovoltaic modules and cells.

It specifies which manufacturers and models can be used in designated government-supported and competitively bid solar projects.

The framework is intended to improve equipment quality, support reliable project performance and encourage the use of domestically manufactured products.

Together, these measures have contributed to the development of India's solar manufacturing ecosystem and strengthened its position in the global renewable energy supply chain.

PM Surya Ghar Expands Rooftop Solar Among Households

Solar energy is increasingly becoming part of everyday household electricity consumption through the PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana.

Launched on February 13, 2024, with an outlay of ₹75,021 crore, the scheme aims to encourage residential rooftop solar installations by providing financial assistance to eligible households.

Collateral-free loans and a dedicated national portal also help consumers complete the registration, installation and subsidy process.

The programme is described as the world's largest domestic rooftop solar initiative.

As of November 2026, approximately 65 lakh households had been solarised under the scheme.

Central Financial Assistance of ₹28,024 crore had been disbursed up to July 2026, while more than 21 lakh households had received zero electricity bills by November 2026.

Rooftop solar enables households to generate their own electricity, reduce dependence on conventional grid power and potentially lower their monthly electricity expenses.

How Rooftop Solar Reduced One Household's Electricity Bills

The experience of Sudhanshu Dubey, a resident of Jawar Colony in Gwalior, highlights the potential financial benefits of residential solar installations.

After learning about PM Surya Ghar, he decided to install a rooftop solar system at his home.

The installation and subsidy process were completed, with the financial assistance credited directly to his bank account.

Before installing the system, his household electricity bill frequently exceeded ₹10,000. After the installation, the monthly bill became almost negligible.

His experience demonstrates how rooftop solar can help households reduce electricity expenses while gaining greater control over their energy consumption.

PM-KUSUM Brings Solar Energy to Indian Farmers

Agriculture is another important area where solar energy is expanding.

Launched in 2019, the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan (PM-KUSUM) promotes solar-powered irrigation and decentralised renewable electricity generation.

The programme supports three major components:

  1. Installation of decentralised solar power plants.
  2. Deployment of standalone solar agricultural pumps.
  3. Solarisation of grid-connected agricultural pumps and electricity feeders.

The scheme aims to provide reliable daytime electricity for irrigation, reduce dependence on diesel-powered pumps and create additional income opportunities for farmers.

Where applicable, farmers can also sell surplus solar electricity to electricity distribution companies.

PM-KUSUM targets approximately 34,800 MW of additional solar capacity by FY 2026–27, supported by central financial assistance of ₹34,422 crore.

As of September 30, 2026, approximately 11.91 lakh standalone off-grid solar pumps had been installed.

More than 17.18 lakh agricultural pumps were covered through feeder-level solarisation, while around 2,426 MW of decentralised solar power capacity had been commissioned on farmers' land.

The programme had benefited more than 21.77 lakh farmers across the country as of December 31, 2025.

Together, PM Surya Ghar and PM-KUSUM are extending solar energy beyond large power plants and creating opportunities for households and agricultural communities.

Distributed solar solutions contributed 16.3 GW, or 36% of India's total 44.61 GW solar capacity addition in FY 2025–26. This included 7.6 GW from PM-KUSUM and 8.7 GW from rooftop solar installations.

India's Role in International Solar Cooperation

India's renewable energy ambitions also include international partnerships, technology exchange and cooperation with developing countries.

International Solar Alliance

Co-founded by India and France at the COP21 climate conference in Paris in 2015, the International Solar Alliance (ISA) has become an important platform for international cooperation in solar energy.

The alliance brings together countries from different regions, including the Maldives, Greece, Germany, Italy, Bhutan, Luxembourg and Argentina.

Its activities focus on accelerating solar deployment, sharing technical knowledge and encouraging investment, particularly across developing economies and the Global South.

One Sun One World One Grid

India proposed the One Sun One World One Grid (OSOWOG) vision in 2018 to promote a connected global renewable electricity network.

In 2021, India and the United Kingdom launched the Green Grids Initiative–One Sun One World One Grid (GGI–OSOWOG) at COP26, with support from Australia, France, the United States and the International Solar Alliance.

The initiative aims to encourage cross-border renewable electricity trade through interconnected power grids.

Such cooperation could help countries share renewable electricity resources, improve energy security and support the global transition towards cleaner energy systems.

During its G20 Presidency, India also helped build consensus around the objective of tripling global renewable energy capacity by 2030.

BRESE 2026 Highlights India's Renewable Energy Opportunities

India's expanding renewable energy sector was showcased at the Bharat Renewable Energy Summit & Expo 2026, held from November 2 to 5 at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi.

Organised under the theme 'Invest. Innovate. Inspire.', the event brought together policymakers, investors, researchers and industry representatives from more than 130 countries.

The summit was held alongside the Ninth International Solar Alliance Assembly and the Fourth International Conference on Green Hydrogen.

Its programme included high-level discussions, ministerial meetings, investment engagements and business networking opportunities.

More than 400 organisations participated in the exhibition, while 13 states showcased their renewable energy potential and investment opportunities as Partner States.

The event also recognised winners of the Bharat Renewable Energy Hackathon 2026, which focused on developing solutions to ten practical challenges in the renewable energy industry.

BRESE 2026 provided an international platform to discuss investment, technological innovation and cooperation in India's growing clean energy sector.

The Road Ahead for India's Solar Industry

India's solar energy expansion reflects the combined impact of government policies, competitive electricity tariffs, domestic manufacturing, infrastructure development and growing public participation.

The increase in installed capacity from 2.82 GW in 2014 to approximately 175 GW in October 2026 highlights the scale of this transformation.

At the same time, rooftop solar, agricultural solarisation, floating photovoltaic projects and battery storage are creating additional opportunities for the sector.

As India works towards its target of 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030 and net-zero emissions by 2070, solar power is expected to remain a central part of its energy strategy.

Beyond electricity generation, the sector has the potential to strengthen energy security, create employment, support domestic industries, reduce carbon emissions and improve access to affordable electricity.

With continued investment, innovation and wider participation from citizens, businesses and farmers, solar energy is becoming an increasingly important contributor to India's sustainable economic development.
